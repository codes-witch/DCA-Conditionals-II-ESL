\section{Introduction}

In recent years, the wide availability of computer-readable data in the form of large corpora has provided linguists with previously inaccessible opportunities for analyses.
Methods from corpus linguistics have been applied to learner data in what is known as Learner Corpus Research (LCR).
This growing field, based on a usage-based perspective of learner language, has contributed to Second Language Acquisition (SLA) and Foreign Language Teaching (FLT) theory 
by describing acquisition processes and comparing learner production with that of native speakers or different proficiency groups \citep{learnercorpusRoemer2026}. 



Many LCR studies have looked into phraseology and collocations in L2 production, finding significant differences with native corpora, with the former underusing low-frequency, highly associated collocations (e.g. \citeauthor{durrantWhatExtentNative2009}, \citeyear{durrantWhatExtentNative2009}; \citeauthor{granger2014use}, \citeyear{granger2014use}).
In a seemingly contradictory manner, linguistic theories rooted in language use sustain that learners of a second language learn through repeated exposure to formulaic constructions such that they are able to extrapolate their meaning though inductive cognitive processes \citep{Ellis_2012}.
With these apparent contradictions in mind, the study of formulaic language in SLA has been an active field of research to which corpus linguistics has made major contributions

In \citet{Ellis_2012}, he cites numerous studies which provide longitudinal evidence for the formulaic nature of L2 learning,
wherein some complex grammatical constructions appear in learners' production in memorized chunks before they have been acquired (for example, \textit{I don't know} before a learner can productively use \textit{do}-support for negation).
In other words, there exists some evidence for lexical variation in constructional slots being indicative of learners' L2 developmental stages.

\subsection{Collostructional analysis}


The present study is pseudo-longitudinal in nature and intends to observe, through a collostructional lense, aspects of the development of the second conditional (henceforth CII) in L2 English learners. 



\begin{questions}
    \item How are CIIs dispersed across learner writing tasks and proficiency level in our analyzed data?\label{rq:disp}
    \item Do advanced learners favor different verbs in the protasis of CIIs than beginner learners?\label{rq:dca}
    \item Does the verbal slot in the protasis of CIIs provide evidence for a transition from more formulaic to more productive use of the construction?\label{rq:lexical_div}
\end{questions}
