\section{Introduction}

In recent years, the wide availability of computer-readable data in the form of large corpora has provided linguists with previously inaccessible opportunities for analyses.
Methods from corpus linguistics have been applied to learner data in what is known as Learner Corpus Research (LCR).
This growing field, based on a usage-based perspective of learner language, has contributed to Second Language Acquisition (SLA) and Foreign Language Teaching (FLT) theory 
by describing acquisition processes and comparing learner production with that of native speakers or different proficiency groups \citep{learnercorpusRoemer2026}. 

Many LCR studies have looked into phraseology and collocations in L2 production, finding significant differences with native corpora, with the former underusing low-frequency, highly associated collocations (e.g. \citeauthor{durrantWhatExtentNative2009}, \citeyear{durrantWhatExtentNative2009}; \citeauthor{granger2014use}, \citeyear{granger2014use}).
In a seemingly contradictory manner, linguistic theories rooted in language use sustain that learners of a second language learn through repeated exposure to formulaic constructions such that they are able to extrapolate their meaning though inductive cognitive processes \citep{Ellis_2012}.
With these apparent contradictions in mind, is is unsurprising that the study of formulaic language in SLA has been an active field of research to which corpus linguistics has made major contributions \citep{McEnery_Brezina_Gablasova_Banerjee_2019}.

\cite{Ellis_2012} cites numerous studies which provide longitudinal evidence for the formulaic nature of L2 learning,
wherein some complex grammatical constructions appear in learners' production in memorized chunks before they have been acquired (for example, \textit{I don't know} before a learner can productively use \textit{do}-support for negation).
In other words, there exists some evidence for lexical variation in constructional slots being indicative of learners' L2 developmental stages.


Although research into L2 collocations is a very active field at the intersection of LCR, SLA and FLT, one corpus linguistics method that (to my knowledge) has largely been ignored in the field of LCR and formulaic sequences is collostructional analysis \citep{stefanowitschCollostructionsInvestigatingInteraction2003,griesExtendingCollostructionalAnalysis2004,griesOverhaulingCollostructionalAnalysis2023}, which investigates the frequency relations and levels of attraction between a lexeme and a slot in a construction.
Such methods may provide insight into the extent to which learner constructions are lexically fixed or productive, thereby offering a potentially useful perspective on developmental processes in SLA.


A construction for which the inductive learning posited to be key in acquisition might pose difficulties is the second conditional (also called conditional type II, henceforth CII).
This structure combines subordination with modality. As such, difficulties in teaching and acquiring have been documented \citep{kang2007difficult, ko2013study}.
Most interestingly, the if-clause (or protasis) in CIIs is often the structure which introduces learners to the past tense form used to indicate subjunctive mood instead of its prototypical temporal value.
The study of protasis verbs in CIIs produced by learners could help shed light on the development of this construction and the acquisition of the English subjunctive in general.
From a usage-based perspective, examining the lexical fillers of the protasis verbal slot may provide insight into the extent to what extent learners rely on formulaic realizations and whether there is evidence for a shift towards productivity.

The present study is pseudo-longitudinal in nature and looks into the use of CIIs in learner texts at two different proficiency levels, zooming in on the attraction strength between verbs and the if-clause verbal slot of CII and its lexical diversity.
Thus, it sets out to explore the following research questions:



\begin{questions}
    \item How are CIIs dispersed across learner writing tasks and proficiency level in our analyzed data?\label{rq:disp}
    \item Do advanced learners favor different verbs in the protasis of CIIs than beginner learners?\label{rq:dca}
    \item Does the verbal slot in the protasis of CIIs provide evidence for a transition from more formulaic to more productive use of the construction?\label{rq:lexical_div}
\end{questions}
